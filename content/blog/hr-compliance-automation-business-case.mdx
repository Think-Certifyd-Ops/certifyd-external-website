The business case for a records tool starts with work your team actually does. Estimate the cost of retrieving evidence, finding its owner and chasing a review before promising that automation will remove it.

## Measure a representative workflow

Choose several recent onboarding or renewal cases. Record the time spent collecting documents, reviewing results, resolving gaps and communicating the decision. Include exceptions rather than measuring only the easiest case.

Distinguish administrative work from a prescribed check or professional judgement. The [Home Office employer guide](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/right-to-work-checks-employers-guide) defines the required checking process. Storing its result does not remove the employer's actions, and reminders cannot make an unresolved decision automatically lawful.

## Make assumptions visible

Estimate case volume, time per case and the team's appropriate cost of time. Label forecasts as assumptions and separate them from measured results. A scenario with six cases saving fifteen minutes each suggests ninety minutes of reduced effort; it is arithmetic, not a customer result or guaranteed saving.

Include implementation, training, data correction and recurring review. A comparison that omits those costs can make a tool look cheaper than the workflow it replaces.

## Evaluate a practical pilot

Agree a bounded group of records and the actions you want to improve. Measure retrieval time, unresolved-action age and the quality of manager handoffs. Check whether exceptions still receive human review and whether sensitive evidence is visible only to appropriate staff.

Keep the result separate from a predicted penalty avoidance figure. A missing record alone is not a finding of illegal working, and invented enforcement probabilities do not establish return on investment.
