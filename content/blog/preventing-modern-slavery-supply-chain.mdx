Modern-slavery work needs attention to how people are recruited and treated. A complete worker document file cannot establish that recruitment fees, threats, coercion or unfair conditions are absent.

## Identify the applicable statement duty

The [transparency-in-supply-chains guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/transparency-in-supply-chains-a-practical-guide) explains which organisations must publish an annual slavery and human trafficking statement and how it should be approved and made available.

Review the actual qualifying conditions rather than assuming every small business has the same statutory publication duty. A statement describes the organisation's steps; publishing it is not certification that no exploitation occurs anywhere in the chain.

## Examine the arrangements behind the labour

Map suppliers and recruitment intermediaries, including who recruits, pays and supervises workers. Ask how workers receive terms, raise concerns and obtain help. Review fees, deductions, accommodation and changes in supplier arrangements where relevant.

Use risk assessment and the current guidance to decide the evidence and follow-up needed. Do not reduce the exercise to checking a supplier logo or collecting the same identity document from every person.

## Make concerns actionable

Give worker concerns a safe reporting route and a responsible reviewer. Preserve relevant evidence, protect sensitive information and follow the applicable safeguarding, emergency or reporting procedure. An internal status change is not the response to a serious concern.

Keep right-to-work responsibility separate. The [employer guide](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/right-to-work-checks-employers-guide) applies to prescribed checking; work permission does not prove that labour conditions are fair. Conversely, missing records need investigation rather than an automatic allegation of trafficking.
