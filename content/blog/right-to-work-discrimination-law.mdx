A consistent policy means treating people fairly and completing the applicable check. It does not mean forcing every person to use an identical document or digital method.

## Apply the policy to the role and process

The [Home Office discrimination code](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/right-to-work-checks-code-of-practice-on-avoiding-discrimination/code-of-practice-for-employers-avoiding-unlawful-discrimination-while-preventing-illegal-working-in-force-from-6-april-2022-accessible-version) explains how employers should avoid unlawful discrimination while preventing illegal working. Plan checks consistently across the relevant workforce, including who conducts them and when, rather than singling out someone because of their name, accent or appearance.

Tell candidates what the check is for and which acceptable routes may apply. Train reviewers to distinguish nationality from permission and to escalate uncertainty without making assumptions about a person's entitlement to work.

## Allow the appropriate evidence route

The [right-to-work guide](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/right-to-work-checks-employers-guide) includes different processes for different circumstances. British and Irish citizens do not all have immigration share codes. A person who is eligible for a manual route should not be rejected simply because an organisation prefers a particular digital workflow.

Keep the decision tied to the evidence and job restrictions. Where a person has an outstanding application or appeal, review the specified checking process instead of assuming an expiry date always means they must stop work immediately.

## Document questions and decisions carefully

Record what evidence was requested, the route used and any review required. Give a person a reasonable opportunity to address a genuine issue within the applicable process. Immigration, employment and discrimination consequences may require advice before an adverse decision.

Restrict access to sensitive documents and record facts rather than comments about perceived nationality. If the policy produces repeated barriers for eligible workers, review the process and provider requirements. Automation can reproduce a poorly designed policy; it is not proof of fair treatment.
