A person who needs support with an unexpected visit should have a simple plan they can use under pressure. The plan should fit their needs and preserve their ability to make decisions with appropriate help.

## Agree how appointments are confirmed

Keep the service provider's established contact details and planned visit information accessible. Agree whom the person can call if the worker or appointment differs. A new number supplied by the visitor should not be the only confirmation route.

Explain that checking is acceptable and that they do not need to give immediate access or payment because a caller sounds urgent. Avoid relying on the person to judge a passport photograph, logo or digital badge unaided.

## Review messages through a trusted route

An email or text about a visit may need checking too. The [NCSC phishing guidance](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/collection/phishing-scams/spot-scams) explains how to handle suspicious communications. Contact the organisation independently instead of following an unexpected link to provide information.

Use an accessible arrangement, such as a clearly written contact card or agreed call to a trusted supporter. Do not circulate sensitive worker identity documents among relatives as a substitute for the provider's own authorisation process.

## Respond to a concern

Where care is provided, contact the provider through its established route to reconcile a changed worker or missed handoff. A serious safeguarding concern needs the appropriate local or provider procedure. Immediate danger needs emergency services.

Keep a factual record of the concern and relevant correspondence where appropriate. Respect the person's privacy and avoid treating age or disability as proof that they cannot decide anything themselves.
